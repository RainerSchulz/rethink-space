## Core thesis in five sentences

Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have made the bridge between the "protein" of the brain and the "silicon" of the computer a reality. What they can read today are motor patterns, not thoughts, and certainly not qualia, the subjective quality of experience. The book argues that consciousness resists measurement for a structural reason: a "neuron-mindspace barrier" that converts objective sensory data into subjective experience in a one-way, irreversible process. This barrier may be an evolutionary protection mechanism that keeps the inner perspective private, so "mind reading" may be a priori impossible. Because BCIs can nonetheless alter identity, emotions and memory, ethical and legal norms must precede mainstream use.

## Structure and chapters

- Introduction: Frames BCIs as bridges between mind and machine that can compromise integrity, identity and responsibility, offers two minimalist definitions of intelligence and places each technology between Bostrom's alarmism and Kurzweil's optimism.
- Chapter 1, Neurological principles and models: Traces BCI history from Berger's EEG (1924) through Vidal's "BMI Challenge" (1973), Nicolelis' thought-controlled robotic arm and Musk's Neuralink. It covers affective BCIs, models of personal identity, body-image illusions, Libet and free will, brain hacking, and localized versus distributed brain function.
- Chapter 2, Data vs. Qualia: The (un)bridgeable valley: Tests the materialist equation "thinking is measurable brain activity". Observed data always need interpretation and qualia can be simulated but never proven; Chalmers' hard problem and a BCI-exoskeleton accident show that BCIs both observe and change what they observe.
- Chapter 3, Why consciousness evades physical measurement: the "neuron-mindspace barrier": Specific to the published edition. Rather than adding another theory of consciousness, it asks why consciousness evades measurement and proposes that the neuron works like a semi-permeable membrane: sensory data pass into Julian Jaynes' "mindspace", are converted into qualia in a pseudo-metabolic change of substrate, and cannot be converted back. The barrier is read as an evolutionary protection of privacy in sedentary societies, which makes breaching it futile and potentially dangerous.
- Chapter 4, Potential ethical challenges and dangers: Discusses extended cognition, the communication gap (Wittgenstein), implanted false memories, the handwriting BCI of Willett et al. (2021) and cyborg artist Neil Harbisson, then proposes a checklist for when a device legally counts as part of the body and describes consent dilemmas for paralyzed, comatose and psychotic patients.
- Chapter 5, Anticipate the future: Treats science fiction as cautionary tales, presents brain-to-brain interfaces (Nicolelis' rats, Rao and Stocco's human link in 2013) and the "Internet of Brains", and follows Metzinger and Madary on VR ethics.
- Conclusions: Humans as toolmakers now shaped by their tools; biological and machine intelligence should complement each other. Covers anthropomorphizing AI as the real near-term threat, BMI-controlled exoskeletons, "fractional time" in simulated memory trips, and a possible two-tier society of augmented and non-augmented humans.

## Key terms and claims

- Protein-silicon bridge: the book's metaphor for any BCI/BMI linking living neural tissue to computers via electrical voltage.
- Neuron-mindspace barrier: the proposed neuronal boundary where objective data become subjective experience and beyond which measurement fails.
- Mindspace: Julian Jaynes' term for the inner sphere where qualia and subjective experience arise.
- Qualia: subjective qualities of experience, known only from the first-person perspective and probably never quantifiable.
- Two definitions of intelligence: biological intelligence draws maximum conclusions from minimum information; machine intelligence draws minimum conclusions from maximum information.
- Affective BCIs: interfaces that detect, influence or stimulate emotions and moods.
- Phenomenal self-model (Metzinger): there is no self, only a transparent, constantly updated and easily manipulated self-model.
- Uncertainty principle of neurophysiology (Nicolelis): neural activity must be measured in space and time together, as a continuum.
- BCI-mediated action: acting through thought alone challenges legal concepts based on intentional body movements.
- Brain hacking: reading, altering, deleting or implanting memories via an interface.
- Internet of Brains (IoB): a hypothetical network of directly connected brains raising privacy and exit questions.
- Fractional time: experiencing weeks or years subjectively in simulation during minutes of real time.

## The author's position

Lierfeld is neither alarmist nor naive optimist. He sees BCIs as a humanistic technology whose life-saving potential ethically obliges us to continue research, but insists on worst-case thinking, informed consent, mental privacy and new legal frameworks before BCIs spread. He follows Metzinger, Carnap and Harari in doubting a fixed self and free will, yet defends the first-person perspective as the only access to qualia. His distinctive stance is epistemic humility: the inaccessibility of consciousness may be a feature, not a bug.

## Relevance for RETHINK SPACE

- Space is only a passing motif: Vidal's 1973 question whether brain signals could control "prostheses or spaceships" (p. 18) and "exploring the galaxy using remote-controlled spaceships" as a BCI application (p. 111).
- BCI-supervised robotics: it traces the line from Nicolelis' robotic arm to BMI-controlled exoskeletons; its exoskeleton thought experiment (who is responsible when a read error causes harm?) applies to any BCI-supervised robot.
- Mission control: Stocco's vision of a person on the ground helping to land a plane via a brain-to-brain link (p. 106) is the closest analogue to remote supervision.
- Dual-use: the book notes the dual-use nature of neurotechnology, its cyber vulnerability, its DARPA origins and hacking by non-state actors.
- It does not discuss space missions; its value is the ethical and epistemic framework.

## Typical questions and answers

- What is the book about? What BCIs can and cannot do, why consciousness evades measurement, and the ethical and legal consequences of bridging brain and computer.
- What is the "neuron-mindspace barrier"? The author's model of a neuronal boundary that converts objective sensory data into subjective experience one way only, so experience cannot be read from outside.
- Can BCIs read minds today? No. Current interfaces decode motor patterns such as imagined handwriting, but thoughts, emotions and qualia remain interpreted estimates.
- Why might consciousness be unmeasurable on purpose? Privacy of thought may have been an evolutionary survival advantage, so the barrier may be a protective mechanism.
- Does the author believe in free will? He follows Libet and Harari in treating free will as largely an illusion, but stresses that BCIs add new deterministic factors.
- Why are affective BCIs problematic? They alter emotions and moods, which constitute the self-model and autobiographical memory, so manipulating them threatens identity and requires strict consent.
- Who is responsible if a BCI-controlled device causes harm? Unclear: the cause may be the user's intention, a read error or a hacked interface, so concepts of agency need revision.
- What is the Internet of Brains? A speculative network of many directly connected brains, extrapolated from real brain-to-brain experiments.
- How does the author define intelligence? Biological intelligence draws a maximum of conclusions from a minimum of information; machine intelligence a minimum from a maximum.
- Is the author for or against BCI technology? For it, under regulation: continued research accompanied by ethics committees, informed consent and new legal frameworks.
